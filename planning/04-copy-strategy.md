## How to use this document

This is the copy source of truth for the Dundir website. Every headline, subheadline, key message, and CTA in this document is ready to paste into a design tool. Do not rewrite or soften the language during design — if copy feels too direct, that is intentional. Dutch construction procurement managers are experienced, skeptical, and time-poor. They have already heard every AI pitch. The copy here is written for them.

For each section, the psychological mechanism is labelled so designers and developers understand what the copy is doing and do not inadvertently break it.

## 1. Positioning Statement

**The factual anchor. Everything else hangs from this.**

> Dundir is a locally deployed procurement decision system for Dutch construction companies and municipalities — it finds the mathematically optimal supplier combination for each procurement need and produces an audit-ready report, without sending any data outside your own infrastructure.

**What this is not:** a tagline. This is the internal compass. Every page, every headline, every CTA should be consistent with this statement. If a claim would not fit inside this statement, it does not belong on the site.

## 2. Hero Headline Options

**Psychological profile of the reader at this moment:**
- Awareness stage: Problem-aware to solution-aware. They know procurement is broken. They are skeptical that AI actually helps.
- Emotional state: Frustrated and time-poor, with growing regulatory anxiety (CPR 2024, CSRD, Aanbestedingswet).
- Trust level: Low toward AI vendors. High toward numbers, specifics, and evidence from their own domain.
- Primary mechanism needed: Problem agitation + proof specificity + credibility signal. Not aspiration. Not transformation language.

---

### Headline A — Loss Framing / Reference Point

**Headline:**
Eleven hours a week. Per buyer. Just to compare suppliers.

**Sub-headline:**
That is €49,500 a year in procurement admin — per person. Not including the cost of the decision that was wrong.

**Psychological mechanism:** Loss aversion via concrete reference point (Kahneman & Tversky). The reader already knows the number is true before they finish the sentence. The sub-headline adds the second loss layer — not just wasted time, but the risk of the wrong outcome. Does not feel manufactured because it is sourced and specific.

**Who it speaks to:** The procurement manager doing the work — someone who feels the 11 hours every week but has never seen it expressed as a number. Also effective for their manager who owns the cost line.

**When to use it:** Default hero for the construction company segment. Highest impact on a first visit from someone who found Dundir through search or a referral.

**Caution:** Pair this with an immediate proof source below the fold. Do not let the statistic stand unattributed.

**Psychological mechanism:** Concrete before/after progress promise (JTBD framing). No metaphor, no provocation, no fear. Just the job done well. This headline works for the buyer who does not need to be convinced the problem exists — they live it — and wants to know if the solution actually works. The three-line sub-headline structure mirrors how a procurement manager thinks: local deployment (data stays), trained on their data (relevant), explainable (defensible).

**Who it speaks to:** The hands-on procurement manager who is already looking for a solution and arrived on the site with intent.

**When to use it:** Retargeting, email campaigns to warm leads, the scan landing page. This is the "ready to convert" headline. Use it lower in the funnel, not cold.

### How It Works

**The one belief:**
Dundir does not replace the procurement manager's judgment — it removes the gruntwork before the judgment and documents the reasoning after it.

**This distinction is critical.** Dutch construction professionals will immediately distrust any framing that sounds like the AI decides. The copy must be precise: the optimizer calculates options, the human reviews and decides, the system documents what was decided and why.

**Proof point:** The Skanska case study (Hamppi, 2025) is the negative proof — it shows what happens when you hand the decision to a generic GenAI tool. A 28% error on a single line item. The deterministic optimizer is the answer to that documented failure mode.

**Copy block — how the system works:**

Step 1: You input a material specification — in plain Dutch, the same way you would write it now.

Step 2: The AI layer reads the specification and translates it into structured procurement inputs: required certifications, delivery windows, budget constraints, weighted objectives.

Step 3: The optimizer runs against your supplier database. It finds the mathematically provable best combination of suppliers given your constraints. Not a suggestion. A calculated answer with full reasoning.

Step 4: You review the output — a ranked comparison with sourced data, certification status, and a complete reasoning trail. You decide. The system records your decision and why.

Step 5: The audit-ready report is generated automatically. Ready for internal approval, external audit, or public procurement review.

Everything runs on your own server. Your pricing data, supplier relationships, and procurement history never leave your infrastructure. The longer you use the system, the more it learns from your own data — and the sharper the optimizer gets.

**What the system does not do:** It does not make procurement decisions. It does not override your judgment. It does not replace supplier relationships. It makes the comparison work faster, more accurate, and legally defensible — and then hands the decision to you.

**Bowtie stage served:** Education → Selection. This page closes the mechanism gap for a visitor who is seriously evaluating.

### Time and cost of manual procurement

**Claim:** The average procurement manager spends 11.2 hours per week on procurement administration.
**Source:** Remarcable / BuildMate 2025 (cited in vpc-a-construction-company.md)
**Exact form for site:** "11 hours a week per procurement manager, on comparison work alone"
**Caution flag:** This figure comes from industry surveys, not peer-reviewed research. Do not present as a scientific finding. Attributing to "industry research" or "procurement benchmarking data" is more accurate than citing a specific study name on the site.

**Claim:** That translates to €49,500 per year per person in lost productive time.
**Source:** Derived from the 11.2 hr/week figure using a fully loaded labour cost. (Remarcable / BuildMate 2025)
**Exact form for site:** "€49,500 a year in procurement admin per buyer — before the cost of errors"
**Caution flag:** This is a derived calculation, not a directly measured figure. The derivation is defensible but should not be presented as a measured outcome. Use as a directional illustration, not a precise benchmark.

**Claim:** A manual bid comparison for 6 subcontractors took 30 minutes; a GenAI version took approximately 2 minutes.
**Source:** Hamppi (2025), p. 65, Table 7 — Skanska case study, single cleaning-services contract
**Exact form for site:** "In Skanska's internal experiment, manual bid comparison: 30 minutes. GenAI: 2 minutes. But the GenAI output required error-checking that eliminated the time saving."
**Caution flag:** This is a single case study on a small cleaning-services contract at one company. Do not extrapolate to structural works, MEP, or multi-million-euro packages. The stat is best used to illustrate the problem with generic GenAI tools, not to make a time-saving claim for Dundir.

**Caution flag:** This was Skanska's proprietary internal LLM tool on a specific type of contract. The Dundir optimizer is architecturally different (deterministic, not generative). ## 5. What NOT to Say

These are phrases and framings that will immediately lose the trust of a Dutch construction procurement manager. They are organized by the specific reason they fail.

---

### Phrases that signal you have never been in a procurement meeting

"Transform your procurement process" — transformation language belongs to business schools, not construction sites. Procurement managers want to solve a specific problem on Thursday afternoon, not transform anything.

"AI-powered insights" — "insights" is a hollow word in this context. What insights? About what? Into what? The word is used by every SaaS tool that could not describe what it actually does.

"Unlock the full potential of your procurement data" — this is a sales deck phrase. Nobody has ever said this in a procurement meeting. It signals immediately that the writer has never been in one.

"Seamlessly integrates with your existing workflow" — "seamlessly" is the most abused word in enterprise software. The integration is never seamless. Say what it actually integrates with and what the implementation involves.

"Future-proof your procurement" — future-proofing is not a procurement goal. Getting the right supplier for the current project, documented and defensible, is a procurement goal.

"Best-in-class" — this is not a claim, it is a noise word. It adds no information and actively signals generic marketing copy.

---

### Generic AI promises that have already failed trust

"Harness the power of AI" — no. This is not 2018.

"AI that learns and adapts" — too vague to mean anything. What does it learn? From what data? Adapts to what? Every ML vendor says this.

"Cutting-edge machine learning" — the word "cutting-edge" in a B2B context reads as compensating for a lack of specific claims. Use the actual technical description (linear optimization + evolutionary algorithms) or do not describe the technology at all.

"Trusted by leading construction companies" — if you cannot name the company, do not make the claim. A Dutch procurement director will ask who. If the answer is a generic "our clients" they will stop reading.

"Our AI understands construction" — this sounds like it was written to be reassuring but lands as defensive. Show that it understands construction through specific domain knowledge, not through the claim itself.

---

### Hollow superlatives and Silicon Valley framing

"Revolutionize" — construction professionals are allergic to this word. The industry has been promised revolution by technology vendors for 30 years. The spreadsheet is still winning.

"Game-changer" — same problem. This is a press release word. It has no meaning in a procurement context.

"Disrupt" — do not use this word with Dutch construction professionals. Ever.

"Empower your team" — empowerment is not a procurement goal. Accuracy, speed, and defensibility are procurement goals.

"We are on a mission to..." — nobody asked about your mission. They asked whether the tool works.

"The future of construction procurement" — this is a positioning statement for a marketing presentation, not for people who need a comparison report before Friday.

---

### Claims that will be challenged and should not be made

Do not claim time savings without qualifying the scope (e.g., "30-minute comparison" refers to a cleaning-services contract in a Skanska case study, not a multi-million-euro structural package).

Do not claim "eliminates errors" — the system reduces calculation errors in comparison work. Human review is still required. The Skanska study shows what happens when you trust a tool without human oversight.

Do not claim "fully automated" — the human reviews and decides. The system prepares and documents. Any framing that implies the system makes the procurement decision will raise immediate red flags with procurement directors and municipality legal teams.

Do not claim market leadership or "the only" without being precise about the scope of the claim. "The only locally deployed, fine-tuned procurement decision system for the Dutch construction market" is a defensible claim. "The leading AI procurement tool" is not.

## 6. CTA Copy

**Principle:** Each CTA should feel like a natural next step, not a conversion event. The language shifts in urgency from the hero position (earliest, most open) through mid-page (reader is engaged and considering) to footer (reader has read everything and is ready or not). Push too hard at the hero and you lose the skeptic. Be too soft at the footer and you lose the ready buyer.

**Psychological mechanism across all CTAs:** autonomy-preserving prompts. The CTA should feel like the reader's choice, not a sales close. Dutch procurement professionals in particular respond badly to pressure — they are trained to evaluate, not to react.

---

### Hero CTA (earliest position — cold visitor)

**Primary button:**
Calculate my procurement cost

**Supporting line below the button:**
Five questions. Specific to your team size and volume. No email required before you see the result.

**What this does:** The calculation framing (not "start a free trial," not "book a demo") matches the reader's mental model — they are about to find out a number that is specific to them. "No email required before you see the result" removes the most common reason someone hesitates before a scan or quiz. It signals transparency and low commitment.

---

### Mid-page CTA (engaged reader — education to selection transition)

**Version 1 — after the problem section:**
Your number is probably higher than the industry average. Find out what it actually is.

Run the procurement scan

**Supporting line:**
Takes five minutes. Specific to your team, your volume, your process.

**Version 2 — after the how-it-works section:**
See how it applies to your procurement setup.

Run the scan — or book a 30-minute call with the team.

**Supporting line:**
No prepared pitch. No deck. We will ask about your current process and tell you whether Dundir is a fit.

**What this does:** Version 1 intensifies personal loss relevance (the industry average does not feel threatening — the personal number does). Version 2 gives two options, which preserves autonomy and captures both the self-serve visitor and the one who prefers direct conversation. The "no prepared pitch" language specifically addresses the skepticism of a procurement professional who expects a sales call.

---

### Footer CTA (bottom of any page — reader who has read everything)

**Primary button:**
Run the procurement scan

**Supporting line:**
Five minutes. Your numbers. A conversation if it makes sense.

**What this does:** By the footer, over-explaining is the failure mode. The reader knows what the scan is. The three-sentence structure is deliberately compressed. "A conversation if it makes sense" is the lowest-pressure version of a lead capture — it implies that we evaluate fit, not just collect leads. That framing is credible for a procurement professional who is also evaluating us.

---

### Scan completion / results CTA (after the scan output)

**Primary button:**
Talk to the team about these numbers

**Supporting line:**
This is your estimated cost. Our team can show you what the delta looks like after a Dundir implementation — with specific reference to your process and team size.

**What this does:** The word "these numbers" anchors the CTA to the specific output the reader just received — not to a generic sales conversation. "Delta" is deliberate domain language that a procurement manager will recognize as familiar. The framing is "what changes for you specifically," not "buy our product."

---

### Email outreach / follow-up CTA (for use in post-scan or post-event outreach)

Subject line option A:
Your procurement cost estimate — and what it looks like after

Subject line option B:
One question about your comparison workflow

Body CTA line:
If the number surprised you, I am happy to walk through what it typically looks like six months into a Dundir implementation — no deck, just your process.

**What this does:** Subject line A follows the scan logically and continues the "your specific number" frame. Subject line B uses curiosity without clickbait — it is specific enough to promise a real conversation and vague enough to require a click. The body CTA reinforces the no-pressure posture and signals that the conversation will be relevant to their situation, not a standard sales flow.

## Appendix: Psychological Mechanisms Applied

A reference for designers and developers who need to understand what the copy is doing.

| Copy element | Mechanism | Why it works here |
|---|---|---|
