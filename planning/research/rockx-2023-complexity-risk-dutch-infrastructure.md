## Core argument

Project complexity assessment -- structured through the Complexity Based Risk Assessment Method (CBRAM) -- can materially improve risk identification and risk register completeness in Dutch infrastructure projects, particularly during the tendering phase, by making tacit knowledge about complexity-risk causal chains explicit and communicable.

## Key findings relevant to the business

- Rijkswaterstaat (the main Dutch soil, road and waterway client) is actively moving away from large DBFM contracts towards risk-sharing contracts, explicitly to make projects "more predictable and manageable for both ourselves and the market, in preparation and execution" (executive summary). This is a structural procurement shift creating new tool demand.
- Dutch infrastructure project complexity is increasing according to most interviewed practitioners (8+ years to 35 years experience). Drivers cited: multifunctional use of urban space, nitrogen regulation constraints, consolidation of work packages (five contracts crammed into one), growing regulatory accountability requirements, and large-scale DBFM maintenance obligations spanning 30-70 years.
- The risk register -- in practice implemented in tools like VISE (Relatics environment) -- is the central daily risk management artifact for Dutch contractors. Practitioners spend significant effort on ownership, allocation, measures and residual risks. A key pain point: "90% more [risk items] fall into business as usual" category, with a small fraction being genuinely novel or project-specific (p. 48, interviewee 1:7).
- Existing risk management tools do not encourage or enable practitioners to model relationships between risks. This is currently done tacitly and by gut feeling: "In all these years, that's basically how it goes. So I have never come across a systematic approach" (p. 43, interviewee 6:21). There is clear unmet demand for a tool that formalises these relationships.
- Risk ownership and allocation were identified as critical failure modes. When a control measure depends on multiple risks, or a risk has no clear owner, it falls through the cracks: "that is precisely the big risk. That you're a bit caught between the cracks" (p. 44, interviewee 1:18).
- The client (opdrachtgever / OG) behaviour is a significant additional source of project complexity that is not captured in standard complexity frameworks. Multiple interviewees flagged client expectation management, contracting authority task maturity, and divergent risk perspectives between contractor and client as complexity drivers.
- Tacit knowledge loss is a structural problem in large Dutch infrastructure projects. Projects worth 500-600 million euros have fewer than 5% of staff who participate from start to finish (p. 44, interviewee 10:19). Formalising risk and complexity knowledge is therefore a systemic need, not just a nice-to-have.
- CBRAM applied to historical project data (not created using CBRAM) still yielded useful complexity-risk insights -- suggesting such a method can be applied retrospectively and to existing datasets (executive summary, p. i).
- Tendering phase is identified as the highest-value moment to apply complexity-risk assessment, with an iterative approach recommended: at least two rounds (after kick-off and again later when knowledge stabilises). This is when the tool has maximum leverage on bid pricing and risk allocation decisions.
- New contracting forms -- two-phase contracting ("twee-fase") and Bouwteam (alliance-style) -- are specifically identified as the right context for collaborative risk exploration between contractors and clients (executive summary, p. ii).

## Useful statistics and data points

- The Zeesluis IJmuiden DBFM project generated a combined risk register of 451 items, of which at minimum 870 unique risks were estimated to exist if duplicates were removed (p. 56). This illustrates the scale of risk data in large Dutch infrastructure projects.
- 10 semi-structured interviews were conducted with practitioners from Van Hattum en Blankevoort and VolkerWessels EPC, spanning: 2x Planner-Risk managers (8+ years), 4x Design managers (8-25 years), 4x Project managers (8-35 years). Interviews lasted 40-75 minutes (appendix C).
- CBRAM was applied to 4 cases: Renovatie Krammersluizen (tendering phase), Selectieve Onttrekking IJmuiden (construction phase), IJboulevard (evaluation phase), Zeetoegang IJmuiden (maintenance phase).
- 201 coded quotations were produced from the thematic analysis across the 10 interviews (appendix C.3).
- Quarterly reports for Zeesluis IJmuiden contained a list of top risks for the contractor, drawn from a combined risk register of 451 items (p. 56).
- Projects worth 50 to 100 million euros typically have a run-time of approximately three years and many people who experience them from start to finish. Projects of 500 to 600 million euros have fewer than 5% of employees who experience them from start to finish (p. 44).

## Methodology

Multiple case study combined with semi-structured interviews and thematic analysis. CBRAM (from Andringa et al. 2022) was applied to 4 Dutch infrastructure projects across different project phases using existing project data (risk registers, risk breakdown structures). The application produced complexity-risk influence diagrams for each project. Separately, 10 semi-structured interviews were conducted with experienced Dutch infrastructure construction practitioners and analysed using a 17-code thematic coding scheme (4 code groups: CBRAM concept, CBRAM in practice, Complexity, Risk management in practice). An expert panel of 4 practitioners validated the improved CBRAM+ model.

## Applicability to our project

**Dutch market context.** The thesis is written entirely from inside the Dutch infrastructure procurement context (Rijkswaterstaat as client, Van Hattum en Blankevoort and VolkerWessels as contractors), making it directly relevant as market evidence. Rijkswaterstaat's strategic shift away from DBFM towards risk-sharing contracts (stated in the executive summary) is directly relevant when pitching to Dutch public infrastructure clients and municipalities.

**Procurement complexity and the tendering phase.** The finding that the tendering phase is the highest-value moment for complexity-risk assessment -- with rapidly evolving knowledge and high decision stakes -- maps precisely onto the use case for an AI-powered bid analysis and procurement decision tool. The iterative complexity assessment loop (CBRAM+) conceptually mirrors what an AI tool should do: update risk and complexity analysis as new project information arrives.

**Risk framing for municipalities and infrastructure organisations.** The thesis documents that even experienced Dutch practitioners do not systematically map risk interdependencies -- they do it tacitly. An AI tool that surfaces these relationships from existing risk register data (as CBRAM does retroactively) has a clear analogue in automated risk clustering, dependency detection, and anomaly flagging in procurement documents.

**Dataset and knowledge base opportunity.** The recommendation to create "a dataset of standard risk factors that belong to certain complexity factors" (p. 60) -- so teams can select from pre-identified risk events rather than identifying them from scratch -- is directly analogous to a training corpus or retrieval-augmented knowledge base for an AI procurement tool. This frames the AI's value as institutionalised domain knowledge, not just automation.

**Client behaviour as complexity.** The finding that client (opdrachtgever) behaviour is an under-captured source of project complexity is useful for the product: a tool that helps contractors model client-side risk drivers would address a gap that even academic frameworks miss.

**Tacit knowledge formalisation.** The thesis consistently frames the core value proposition as making tacit expert knowledge explicit and communicable. This is also the core value proposition of applying LLMs to domain-specific procurement data -- the framing is directly reusable in pitches.

## Limitations / caveats

- The research is conducted entirely from the contractor perspective (Van Hattum en Blankevoort / VolkerWessels), and does not represent the client (Rijkswaterstaat or municipal) perspective on risk. Do not cite this as evidence of what clients want; it documents what contractors experience.
- The CBRAM framework itself is taken from Andringa et al. (2022) and is not validated beyond 5 applications total (1 original + 4 in this thesis). The improved CBRAM+ model has not been applied to any new project -- it remains a proposal.
- The causal direction of complexity to risk is explicitly questioned in the thesis (p. 55, section 8.1): not every risk was unambiguously traceable to a high-scoring complexity element. CBRAM's fundamental assumption (complexity causes risk) is challenged by the data. Do not cite this thesis as proof that complexity-based risk models are validated.
- All 10 interview participants were employees of Van Hattum en Blankevoort or VolkerWessels EPC -- a single corporate family. This limits generalisability to smaller Dutch contractors or to the public client side.
- The complexity questionnaire was noted by multiple interviewees to use language from a different sector than Dutch infrastructure specifically (p. 41, interviewee 6:1), raising validity concerns for the TOE complexity scoring as applied in the Dutch infra context.
- The thesis focuses on civil/hydraulic infrastructure (sea locks, tunnels, waterways) rather than housing or residential construction. Risk dynamics in housing procurement may differ substantially.
- No quantitative validation of the CBRAM+ model is provided; the expert panel involved only 4 people across 2 sessions of approximately one hour each.
